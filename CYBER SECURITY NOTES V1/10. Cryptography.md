# Cryptography

Encryption matters because it secures data and information from unauthorized access, and in doing so it maintains confidentiality. Cryptography is a much bigger subject than just encryption though, and this note walks through the theory, the algorithms, the attacks, and the practical commands you need to actually use it in a cybersecurity context.

## What Is Cryptography?

Cryptography is the practice and study of techniques for securing communication and data in the presence of adversaries. It is not just about hiding information, it is about guaranteeing certain properties of that information even when someone hostile is actively trying to read it, change it, or impersonate someone else. Everything in this note, from a Caesar cipher to AES-256 to TLS certificates, is really just different ways of achieving those guarantees.

## Key Terms

Before going further it helps to nail down the vocabulary, because these words get mixed up constantly in casual conversation.

Plaintext is the original, readable data before anything is done to it. It does not have to be text, it could be a photo, a video, or any file. Ciphertext is what you get after plaintext has been through an encryption process, and it should look like random noise to anyone without the key. A cipher is simply the method or algorithm used to turn plaintext into ciphertext and back again. Modern ciphers are cryptographic in nature, but historically there were many non-cryptographic ciphers, the Caesar cipher being the classic example.

Encryption is the act of transforming plaintext into ciphertext using a cipher, so that only someone with the right key can reverse the process. Encoding is a completely different thing and is not a form of encryption at all, it is just a way of representing data in another format, like Base64. Encoding is reversible by anyone because the scheme is public, so it gives you no security on its own.

A key is the piece of secret information needed to correctly encrypt or decrypt data. A passphrase is different from a key, it behaves more like a password and is used to protect a key, for example encrypting a private key file so that even if someone steals the file they still cannot use it without the passphrase.

Symmetric encryption uses the same key for both encryption and decryption. Asymmetric encryption uses two mathematically related but different keys, one for encryption and one for decryption. An algorithm is the set of rules or instructions that define how the encryption process actually works, while key exchange is the method used to safely share cryptographic keys between a sender and a recipient, which is a tricky problem in itself since the channel used to exchange the key might not be secure.

Brute force is an attack technique where every possible key or password is tried until the correct one is found, and cryptanalysis is the broader academic and practical study of analyzing cryptographic systems to find weaknesses. Cryptanalysis is where techniques like brute force, dictionary attacks, and rainbow table attacks originate from, and how successful an attacker is at cryptanalysis usually comes down to three resources: time, computing power, and storage.

## Principles of Cryptography

Good cryptography is built around a handful of core goals rather than just one. Confidentiality means information can only be accessed by the person it was intended for. Integrity means information cannot be modified in storage or in transit without the change being detected. Non-repudiation means the sender of a piece of information cannot later deny having sent it. Authentication means the identities of both the sender and receiver, and the origin of the data itself, can be confirmed. Beyond these four, cryptography also aims for interoperability, so that secure communication can happen between different systems and platforms, and adaptability, since cryptographic systems have to keep evolving as computing power grows and new attacks are discovered.

A useful related idea here is Kerckhoffs's principle, which states that a cryptographic system should remain secure even if everything about it is public knowledge except the key. In other words, security should never depend on keeping the algorithm secret, it should depend entirely on keeping the key secret. This is why modern algorithms like AES and RSA are published openly and reviewed by the entire security community, and why a home-grown "secret" encryption scheme is almost always weaker than a well-known public one.

## Encoding, Hashing, and Encryption

These three terms are often confused so it is worth spelling out exactly how they differ before diving into specifics.

Encoding changes data into a new format using a publicly known and easily reversible scheme. It is used to make sure data can be safely transmitted or stored in a format a system expects, not to protect it. Base64 is the classic example. Imagine a user uploading a resume as a .docx file to a job site, the file might get Base64 encoded purely so it can be safely embedded in a request, then decoded back to .docx on the server side for storage. At no point does this protect the resume's contents from anyone who intercepts it, since decoding Base64 takes no special knowledge.

Hashing is a one way process that transforms data into a fixed length string called a hash, digest, or message digest. It cannot be reversed, there is no key involved, and it is mainly used to verify integrity or to store credentials safely. If Alice sends Bob a file along with its hash, Bob can hash the downloaded file himself and compare it to the value Alice sent, and if they match he knows the file was not altered in transit. Hashing is also the recommended way to store passwords, you never store the password itself, only a hash of it combined with a random salt. A salt is a random value appended to the password before hashing, unique to each user, so that even identical passwords produce different stored hashes. When a user logs in, the server hashes their submitted password with the stored salt and compares the result to the stored hash. If a breach occurs, the attacker gets hashes and salts, not passwords, and salting also stops an attacker from noticing that two users share the same password.

Encryption is the process of securely transforming data so that only someone with the correct key or password can reverse it and recover the plaintext. Encryption always implies security, whereas encoding never does. There are two broad types, symmetric key encryption, where the same key encrypts and decrypts, and public key (asymmetric) encryption, where different keys are used for each direction. AES-256 is a widely used example of symmetric encryption, using a 256-bit key, which means there are 2^256 possible keys, an astronomically large number that makes brute forcing the key space infeasible with current technology.

To summarize the difference in one line each: encoding is a reversible format change with no security, hashing is a one way fingerprint used to check integrity or store credentials, and encryption is a reversible but secured transformation used to protect confidentiality.

## Types of Cryptography

Cryptography is broadly split into classic cryptography and modern cryptography.

Classic cryptography refers to methods used before computers existed, and while none of these are secure today, they are still important for understanding the foundations of substitution and transposition, and they still show up in CTFs and puzzles. The Caesar cipher shifts every letter by a fixed number of positions in the alphabet. The Vigenere cipher is a polyalphabetic version of this, using a keyword to apply a different shift to each letter, which makes frequency analysis much harder than with a simple Caesar shift. The Playfair cipher encrypts pairs of letters at a time using a 5x5 grid built from a keyword. The rail fence cipher is a transposition cipher that writes the message in a zigzag pattern across multiple rows and then reads it off row by row to produce the ciphertext. Transposition ciphers in general work by rearranging the order of characters rather than substituting them for different ones. The Atbash cipher simply replaces each letter with its mirror image in the alphabet, so A becomes Z, B becomes Y, and so on.

Modern cryptography covers everything developed from the mid-20th century onward, once computers made much more complex mathematics practical. This includes symmetric key cryptography, where a single secret key handles both encryption and decryption, with AES being the current standard. It includes asymmetric key cryptography, where a public and private key pair is used, with RSA being the most common example. It includes hash functions, which generate fixed size digests used for integrity checking and as building blocks for digital signatures. It includes digital signatures themselves, which combine asymmetric encryption and hashing to provide authentication and integrity. And it includes key exchange algorithms such as Diffie-Hellman and its elliptic curve variant ECDH, which let two parties agree on a shared secret over a channel that might be completely insecure and being watched by an attacker.

## Symmetric Key Cryptography

In symmetric key cryptography, the sender and receiver share a single common key that is used both to encrypt and to decrypt the message. This is fast and efficient, which is why it is used for bulk data encryption, but it has one major weakness, both parties need to somehow agree on the same key beforehand, and doing that securely over an insecure channel is a hard problem on its own, which is exactly what key exchange algorithms like Diffie-Hellman were invented to solve.

Symmetric ciphers themselves come in two flavors, stream ciphers and block ciphers. A stream cipher encrypts data one bit or byte at a time, and because of how the keystream is generated, the same plaintext byte will encrypt to a different ciphertext byte every time, even within the same message. A block cipher instead processes data in fixed size chunks, or blocks, applying a deterministic algorithm along with the symmetric key to each block. AES, the most common block cipher today, works on 128-bit blocks with a key length of 128, 192, or 256 bits. Block ciphers are designed to behave like a pseudorandom permutation, meaning their output should be indistinguishable from a truly random permutation of the input, and they are trusted precisely because nobody has found a practical way to distinguish AES output from randomness.

Two closely related classic techniques worth knowing are transposition and substitution, since block and stream ciphers are really just modern, mathematically rigorous descendants of them. A transposition cipher shifts the positions of the units of plaintext, commonly individual characters, according to a fixed system, so the ciphertext ends up being a rearranged permutation of the original plaintext rather than a set of different symbols. A substitution cipher instead replaces units of plaintext with different units according to a fixed system, the unit could be a single letter, a pair, or larger groups.

### Block Cipher Modes of Operation

A block cipher only tells you how to encrypt one fixed size block, but real data is rarely exactly that size, so a mode of operation defines how to chain many blocks together securely. This is an important topic that is easy to overlook. ECB, or Electronic Codebook, encrypts every block independently with the same key, which is simple but insecure for most real data because identical plaintext blocks always produce identical ciphertext blocks, leaking patterns in the data. CBC, or Cipher Block Chaining, XORs each plaintext block with the previous ciphertext block before encrypting it, which hides patterns but requires an initialization vector and does not parallelize well for encryption. CTR, or Counter mode, turns a block cipher into a stream cipher by encrypting an incrementing counter value and XORing the result with the plaintext, which allows parallel encryption and decryption. GCM, or Galois/Counter Mode, builds on CTR mode and additionally provides authentication, so it detects if the ciphertext has been tampered with, which is why GCM is the recommended mode for most modern applications including TLS.

## Asymmetric Key Cryptography

Also known as public key cryptography, asymmetric encryption uses two different keys, a public key and a private key, that are mathematically linked so that data encrypted with one can only be decrypted with the other. The public key can be freely shared with anyone, while the private key must be kept secret by its owner. This solves the key distribution problem that symmetric encryption suffers from, since you never need to share a secret key over an insecure channel, you just publish your public key.

RSA, named after its inventors Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, is the most widely used asymmetric algorithm. RSA keys are commonly generated at 2048 or 4096 bits, and the public and private keys are mathematically interchangeable in the sense that either could technically play either role, though in practice one is always kept private and one is always shared.

Diffie-Hellman is a different kind of asymmetric technique, it is not used to encrypt data directly, it is used purely to let two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel, even if an eavesdropper is watching every message exchanged. Each party generates a private value, derives a public value from it, and exchanges public values with the other party. Each side then combines their own private value with the other party's public value, and thanks to the underlying mathematics, both sides end up with the same shared secret, which can then be used as a symmetric key. ECDH, Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman, does the same thing but is built on elliptic curve mathematics rather than modular exponentiation, giving equivalent security with much smaller key sizes.

Elliptic Curve Cryptography, or ECC, is worth understanding on its own because it has largely replaced RSA in newer systems. ECC provides the same level of security as RSA but with dramatically smaller keys, for example a 256-bit ECC key is roughly as strong as a 3072-bit RSA key. Smaller keys mean faster computations and less bandwidth used exchanging them, which is why ECC is favored in mobile devices, TLS, and modern SSH.

## Practical: Symmetric Encryption with OpenSSL

To generate a random symmetric key, use the following command.

```bash
openssl rand -out symmetric.key 32
```

Here `rand` generates random bytes, `-out symmetric.key` writes the output to a file, and `32` is the number of bytes to generate, which equals 256 bits, matching an AES-256 key.

To encrypt a file with that key, run:

```bash
openssl enc -aes-256-cbc -salt -pbkdf2 -in plaintext.txt -out encrypted-text.txt -pass file:symmetric.key
```

The `enc` subcommand invokes the encryption utility, `-aes-256-cbc` selects AES with a 256-bit key in CBC mode, `-salt` adds a random salt so the same plaintext does not always produce the same ciphertext, `-pbkdf2` derives the actual encryption key using PBKDF2, a key derivation function that makes brute forcing weak passwords slower, `-in` specifies the input file, `-out` specifies the output file, and `-pass file:symmetric.key` tells OpenSSL to read the passphrase from that key file rather than typing it interactively.

To decrypt, use the same command with `-d` added for decrypt mode:

```bash
openssl enc -d -aes-256-cbc -pbkdf2 -in encrypted-text.txt -out decrypted.txt -pass file:symmetric.key
```

To inspect the raw bytes of the key file in hexadecimal, use:

```bash
xxd symmetric.key
```

## Practical: Asymmetric Encryption with OpenSSL

First generate an RSA private key:

```bash
openssl genrsa -out private_key.pem 2048
```

This creates a 2048-bit RSA private key and saves it to `private_key.pem`.

Next, extract the matching public key from it:

```bash
openssl rsa -in private_key.pem -out public_key.pem -pubout
```

Here `-in` reads the private key, `-pubout` tells OpenSSL to output the public key instead of the private one, and `-out` saves it.

To encrypt a file using the public key:

```bash
openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -inkey public_key.pem -pubin -in plaintext.txt -out encrypted.asym
```

The `-pubin` flag tells OpenSSL that the key given with `-inkey` is a public key rather than a private one, since by default OpenSSL expects a private key.

To decrypt it back using the private key:

```bash
openssl pkeyutl -decrypt -inkey private_key.pem -in encrypted.asym -out decrypted-text.txt
```

## Practical: Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange with OpenSSL

You can actually simulate a Diffie-Hellman exchange locally to see how it works. First generate the shared DH parameters:

```bash
openssl dhparam -out dhparams.pem 2048
```

Then generate a private/public key pair for each party using those parameters:

```bash
openssl genpkey -paramfile dhparams.pem -out alice_private.pem
openssl pkey -in alice_private.pem -pubout -out alice_public.pem

openssl genpkey -paramfile dhparams.pem -out bob_private.pem
openssl pkey -in bob_private.pem -pubout -out bob_public.pem
```

Finally, each side derives the same shared secret using their own private key and the other party's public key:

```bash
openssl pkeyutl -derive -inkey alice_private.pem -peerkey bob_public.pem -out shared_secret_alice.bin
openssl pkeyutl -derive -inkey bob_private.pem -peerkey alice_public.pem -out shared_secret_bob.bin
```

Comparing `shared_secret_alice.bin` and `shared_secret_bob.bin` will show they are identical, even though neither party ever transmitted their private key or the final secret itself.

## Hash Functions

A hash function is a mathematical algorithm that takes an input of any size and returns a fixed size string of bytes called a hash value, message digest, or digest for short. Unlike encryption, there is no key involved, and it is meant to be computationally infeasible to reverse the process and recover the original input from the output. A good hash function is fast to compute in the forward direction but prohibitively slow to reverse, and even a single bit change in the input should produce a drastically different output.

Hashing serves several purposes in cryptographic systems. It is used for data integrity, since comparing the hash of received data against a known good hash reveals whether the data was altered. It is used for password storage, where the system stores the hash of a password rather than the password itself, then hashes whatever is submitted at login time and compares the results. It is used in digital signatures, where a message is hashed before the hash itself is signed with a private key, which is far more efficient than signing the entire message. And it is used in Message Authentication Codes such as HMAC, which combine hashing with a secret key to authenticate messages.

To be considered cryptographically secure, a hash function needs several properties. It must be deterministic, meaning the same input always produces the same output. It must be fast to compute. It must have pre-image resistance, meaning given a hash output it should be infeasible to find any input that produces it. It must have second pre-image resistance, meaning given one input and its hash, it should be infeasible to find a different input that produces the same hash. It must have collision resistance, meaning it should be hard to find any two different inputs that produce the same hash at all, since a collision undermines the trustworthiness of the whole function. And it should exhibit the avalanche effect, where a tiny change in input causes a large, unpredictable change in output.

### HMAC

HMAC, or Hash-based Message Authentication Code, deserves its own mention because it is one of the most common uses of hashing in real systems and is often confused with plain hashing. A regular hash function on its own does not prove who created the hash, anyone could compute the hash of a message. HMAC solves this by combining a hash function with a secret key shared between sender and receiver, so the resulting code proves both that the message has not been altered and that it was produced by someone who knows the secret key. HMAC is used extensively in API authentication, JWT signing, and network protocols like IPsec and TLS.

You can compute an HMAC with OpenSSL like this:

```bash
openssl dgst -sha256 -hmac "supersecretkey" -in message.txt
```

This produces the HMAC-SHA256 of `message.txt` using the given key, and anyone with the same key can recompute it to verify the message's authenticity and integrity.

## Rainbow Table Attacks

A rainbow table attack is a technique that uses precomputed tables of plaintext-to-hash mappings to reverse hashed passwords quickly, without brute forcing every possibility on the spot. The attacker obtains a hashed password, looks it up in the table, and if there is a match, recovers the original plaintext almost instantly.

The defense against this is salting. A salt is a random string of characters added to a password before it is hashed, unique per user, which means an attacker would need a separate rainbow table for every possible salt value, making the attack impractical. Hash functions designed for password storage, such as bcrypt and scrypt, handle salting automatically. Salts do not need to be kept secret, their value comes purely from being unique, not from being hidden.

A secure password storage scheme generally looks like this: pick a strong password hashing function such as Argon2, Scrypt, Bcrypt, or PBKDF2, generate a unique random salt for the user, concatenate the password and salt together, hash the combined string, and store both the resulting hash and the salt. It is worth noting why encryption is not used instead of hashing for password storage, if you encrypted passwords you would need to store the decryption key somewhere, and anyone who obtained that key could decrypt every stored password at once, which defeats the purpose entirely.

## Popular Hash Algorithms

| Algorithm  | Output Size (bits) | Hex Digits | Notes |
|------------|--------------------|------------|-------|
| MD5        | 128                | 32         | Cryptographically broken, still used for basic checksums only |
| SHA-1      | 160                | 40         | Cryptographically broken since the 2017 SHAttered collision |
| SHA-224    | 224                | 56         | Part of the SHA-2 family |
| SHA-256    | 256                | 64         | Part of SHA-2, the most widely used general purpose hash today |
| SHA-384    | 384                | 96         | Part of the SHA-2 family |
| SHA-512    | 512                | 128        | Part of SHA-2, offers high security margin |
| SHA-3      | 224 / 256 / 384 / 512 | Variable | Keccak-based, structurally different from SHA-2 |
| BLAKE2     | Up to 512          | Variable   | Fast, secure, widely used in modern software |
| BLAKE3     | Arbitrary          | Arbitrary  | Newer, extremely fast, parallelizable |
| RIPEMD-160 | 160                | 40         | Used in Bitcoin address generation alongside SHA-256 |
| Whirlpool  | 512                | 128        | Based on a modified AES-like construction |

MD5 was developed by Ronald Rivest in 1991 and was one of the first widely adopted hash algorithms, producing a 128-bit digest. It was originally used to check that downloaded files had not been corrupted or tampered with. In 2004 researchers demonstrated the first practical MD5 collision, meaning two different inputs that produced the same hash, and by 2008 generating such collisions had become cheap and fast. MD5 is now considered cryptographically broken and should never be used anywhere security matters, though it still lingers in non-security checksumming.

SHA-1 was developed by the NSA and published by NIST in 1995, producing a 160-bit digest. For years it was the backbone of SSL/TLS, PGP, and Git. Theoretical weaknesses were found around 2005, and in 2017 Google and CWI Amsterdam demonstrated the SHAttered attack, a real practical collision using two different PDF files, proving SHA-1 was broken in practice, not just in theory. It is now deprecated across all major standards.

The SHA-2 family was designed as SHA-1's successor and consists of six variants, SHA-224, SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512, SHA-512/224, and SHA-512/256, with the number referring to the output length in bits. SHA-256 in particular is extremely widely used, appearing in TLS certificates, Bitcoin, PGP signatures, and general file verification. No practical collision attack has ever been found against SHA-2. Its one downside is that it is relatively heavy to compute on constrained hardware like smart cards, which is part of what motivated SHA-3.

SHA-3, published by NIST in 2015, is not built on the same design as SHA-1 or SHA-2, it uses the Keccak sponge construction instead of the older Merkle-Damgard construction. It was created as a backup standard in case a fundamental flaw was ever found in SHA-2, rather than because SHA-2 was broken. It is considered very secure but has not seen the same widespread adoption as SHA-2, mainly because SHA-2 continues to work fine.

RIPEMD is a family of hash functions developed in the mid-1990s in Europe, and the most popular variant, RIPEMD-160, produces a 160-bit hash using two parallel lines of computation, which structurally differentiates it from MD5 and SHA-1. It is still used in Bitcoin address generation alongside SHA-256 but has otherwise been overtaken by the SHA family.

Whirlpool was designed by Vincent Rijmen, one of the co-creators of AES, and Paulo Barreto, producing a 512-bit hash based on an AES-like Substitution-Permutation Network. Its long output makes it suitable for high security applications, but it is slower and less widely supported than SHA-2.

BLAKE was a finalist in the competition that eventually chose Keccak for SHA-3, and it went on to spawn BLAKE2, a fast and secure alternative to MD5, SHA-1, and SHA-2 that is popular in systems like IPFS and various blockchains, and BLAKE3, released in 2020, which uses a tree structure that makes it extremely fast on multi-core hardware and well suited to hashing large files.

For password hashing specifically, general purpose hash functions like SHA-256 are actually a poor choice, because they are designed to be fast, and fast hashing is exactly what makes brute forcing easier. Bcrypt is based on the Blowfish cipher and introduces a configurable cost factor that controls how slow it deliberately is, and it handles salting automatically, it is proven and widely used but does not resist GPU-accelerated cracking as well as newer options. Scrypt builds on the same idea but adds memory-hardness, meaning it also requires a large amount of memory to compute, which makes it expensive to parallelize on GPUs or ASICs. Argon2 is the current recommended standard for password hashing, and comes in three variants, Argon2d which resists GPU cracking, Argon2i which resists side-channel attacks, and Argon2id which is a hybrid of both and is generally the recommended default.

## Recognising Password Hashes

Automated tools like hashID exist to guess a hash's algorithm from its format, but they are unreliable for many hash types since several algorithms produce output of the same length and character set. Context usually matters more than the tool, for instance a hash pulled from a web application's database is far more likely to be MD5 than NTLM, even though the two can look superficially similar.

On Linux, password hashes are stored in `/etc/shadow`, which is only readable by root, unlike the historic `/etc/passwd` file which was world readable. Each line in the shadow file has nine colon-separated fields, the first two being the username and the encrypted password. The encrypted password field is structured as `$prefix$options$salt$hash`, where the prefix identifies which algorithm was used.

| Prefix | Algorithm |
|--------|-----------|
| `$y$` | yescrypt, the modern default on most current Linux distributions |
| `$gy$` | gost-yescrypt, combining the GOST hash with yescrypt |
| `$7$` | scrypt |
| `$2b$`, `$2y$`, `$2a$`, `$2x$` | bcrypt, based on the Blowfish cipher |
| `$6$` | sha512crypt, based on SHA-512 |
| `$md5` | SunMD5, originally developed for Solaris |
| `$1$` | md5crypt, originally developed for FreeBSD |

On Windows, passwords are hashed using NTLM, which is a variant of MD4, and the hashes are stored in the SAM database (Security Accounts Manager). Windows tries to prevent normal users from reading this database directly, but tools such as Mimikatz exist to dump these hashes from a compromised system. Windows historically also used the much weaker LM hash format alongside NT hashes. NTLM hashes are visually identical to MD4 and MD5 hashes, so context is essential to correctly identify them.

## Password Cracking Practical

Two tools dominate password cracking, John the Ripper and Hashcat. John the Ripper is CPU based and very convenient for quick jobs and format autodetection, while Hashcat is GPU accelerated and much faster for large scale cracking.

To crack a hash with John the Ripper using a wordlist, run:

```bash
john --wordlist=/usr/share/wordlists/rockyou.txt hashes.txt
```

To see cracked passwords afterward:

```bash
john --show hashes.txt
```

To run Hashcat against the same wordlist, you need to know the hash mode number for the algorithm in question, for example mode 0 is MD5, mode 1000 is NTLM, and mode 1800 is sha512crypt.

```bash
hashcat -m 1000 -a 0 hashes.txt /usr/share/wordlists/rockyou.txt
```

Here `-m 1000` sets the hash type to NTLM, `-a 0` selects a straight wordlist attack, and the remaining arguments are the hash file and the wordlist. To view already cracked results:

```bash
hashcat -m 1000 hashes.txt --show
```

## Applications of Cryptographic Algorithms

### Digital Signatures

A digital signature is a cryptographic technique used to verify both the authenticity and the integrity of digital data or a message, and it relies entirely on asymmetric encryption. To create a digital signature, the sender first hashes the message to produce a fixed length digest, then encrypts that digest with their own private key, and this encrypted digest is the signature. To verify a signature, the receiver decrypts the signature using the sender's public key to recover the original hash, independently computes the hash of the message they actually received, and compares the two. If they match, the receiver knows the message was not altered and that it genuinely came from someone holding the corresponding private key.

To confirm GPG and OpenSSL are installed:

```bash
gpg --version
openssl version
```

If not, they can be installed with:

```bash
sudo apt update && sudo apt install gnupg openssl
```

### Digital Signatures Practical with OpenSSL

First generate a data file and hash it:

```bash
echo 'data to sign' > my_data.txt
openssl dgst -sha256 my_data.txt
```

Generate a private and public key pair:

```bash
openssl genrsa -out private.pem 2048
openssl rsa -in private.pem -out public.pem -outform PEM -pubout
```

Sign the file using the private key, producing a binary signature file:

```bash
openssl dgst -sha256 -sign private.pem -out signature.bin my_data.txt
```

Verify the signature using the public key:

```bash
openssl dgst -sha256 -verify public.pem -signature signature.bin my_data.txt
```

### Digital Signatures Practical with GPG

Generate a new key pair interactively:

```bash
gpg --full-generate-key
```

You will be asked to choose a key type, RSA and RSA is recommended, a key size, 2048 or 4096 bits, an expiration date, and a name, email address, and passphrase to protect the private key.

List your keys:

```bash
gpg --list-keys
```

Sign a file, which produces a combined, non-readable output file:

```bash
gpg --sign my_data.txt
```

This creates `my_data.txt.gpg`. To produce a detached signature instead, which keeps the original file untouched and creates a separate signature file:

```bash
gpg --detach-sign my_data.txt
```

This creates `my_data.txt.sig`.

Export your public key so others can verify your signatures:

```bash
gpg --export --armor "Your Name or Email" > public_key.asc
```

Verify a signed file:

```bash
gpg --verify my_data.txt.sig my_data.txt
```

### Encrypting and Decrypting with GPG

Beyond signing, GPG can also encrypt files for a specific recipient using their public key. First import the recipient's public key if you do not already have it:

```bash
gpg --import recipient_public_key.asc
```

Encrypt a file for that recipient:

```bash
gpg --encrypt --recipient "recipient@example.com" secret.txt
```

This produces `secret.txt.gpg`, which only the holder of the matching private key can decrypt:

```bash
gpg --decrypt secret.txt.gpg > secret.txt
```

Digital signatures matter because they provide authentication, confirming the identity of the sender, integrity, confirming the message was not altered in transit, and non-repudiation, since only the holder of the private key could have produced a valid signature, meaning the sender cannot later deny sending it. This is used everywhere from securing emails and software distribution, to legally binding document signing, to validating blockchain transactions.

### Digital Certificates

A digital certificate is an electronic document that proves ownership of a public key, essentially acting as a digital passport that links a public key to the identity of a person, organization, or server. Certificates are issued by trusted third parties called Certificate Authorities, or CAs. A certificate typically contains the holder's identity information, their public key, details of the issuing CA, a validity period, a serial number, and the CA's own digital signature over the whole certificate, which is what allows anyone to verify the certificate has not been tampered with.

To get a certificate, an entity generates a key pair, submits the public key along with identity details to a CA for verification, and once verified the CA issues a signed certificate. When someone later receives that certificate, they can verify it using the CA's own public key, and if it checks out, they can trust both the identity of the holder and the public key itself. This is the foundation of HTTPS, where any modern website should have a TLS certificate, either paid for from a commercial CA or obtained for free from Let's Encrypt.

### Digital Certificates Practical

Generate a private key:

```bash
openssl genrsa -out private.key 2048
```

Generate a Certificate Signing Request, which will prompt for details like country, state, city, organization, common name, and email:

```bash
openssl req -new -key private.key -out request.csr
```

Create a self-signed certificate valid for one year:

```bash
openssl x509 -req -days 365 -in request.csr -signkey private.key -out certificate.crt
```

Inspect the resulting certificate's contents:

```bash
openssl x509 -in certificate.crt -text -noout
```

A self-signed certificate works for local testing but is not trusted by browsers, since it was not vouched for by a recognized CA, real world sites need a certificate signed by a trusted CA instead.

### Inspecting a Live Website's TLS Certificate

You can check the certificate a remote server is actually presenting, without needing a browser, using OpenSSL's `s_client`:

```bash
openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com </dev/null 2>/dev/null | openssl x509 -text -noout
```

This connects to the server, retrieves the certificate it presents during the TLS handshake, and prints its details, which is a handy way to check expiry dates, the issuing CA, and which domains a certificate actually covers.

### The TLS Handshake, Briefly

It is worth understanding what actually happens when a browser connects to an HTTPS site, since it ties together certificates, asymmetric encryption, and symmetric encryption into one flow. The client and server first agree on which TLS version and cipher suite to use. The server then presents its certificate, and the client verifies it against a trusted CA. Next, the client and server perform a key exchange, commonly (EC)DHE in modern TLS, to agree on a shared secret without ever transmitting it directly. That shared secret is then used to derive symmetric session keys, and from that point on all the actual data is encrypted using fast symmetric encryption like AES-GCM, with the earlier asymmetric cryptography only having been used to safely set up that shared secret. This hybrid approach exists because asymmetric encryption is too slow to use for bulk data, while symmetric encryption alone cannot solve the key distribution problem, combining the two gets the security of one with the performance of the other.

A related concept is Perfect Forward Secrecy. When TLS uses an ephemeral key exchange such as DHE or ECDHE, meaning a fresh key pair is generated for every single session rather than reusing a long-term key, then even if an attacker later steals the server's private key, they cannot decrypt past recorded sessions, because those sessions were never actually protected by that long-term key in the first place, only by the temporary session key which is discarded afterward.

## SSH

SSH, Secure Shell, is a cryptographic network protocol for secure communication over an untrusted network, and it uses several of the concepts already covered here in combination.

When a client connects to an SSH server for the first time, the server presents its public key, and the client stores it in `~/.ssh/known_hosts`. On every future connection, the client checks that the server's key still matches what was stored, and if it suddenly changes, the client warns loudly, since this could indicate a man-in-the-middle attack, though it could also just mean the server was legitimately reinstalled.

Clients can authenticate to the server in two main ways. Password authentication is simple but vulnerable to brute force attempts. Public key authentication is far more secure, the client proves it holds the private key corresponding to a public key already trusted by the server, without ever transmitting the private key itself.

SSH key pairs are generated with `ssh-keygen`, which supports several algorithms. DSA is an older digital signature algorithm rarely used today. ECDSA is an elliptic curve variant of DSA offering smaller keys for equivalent security. Ed25519 is a modern signature scheme built on Curve25519 and is generally the recommended choice today for its combination of strong security, small key size, and fast performance. Both ECDSA-SK and Ed25519-SK are variants that support storing the key on a hardware security key for extra protection against theft.

To generate a modern Ed25519 key pair:

```bash
ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "your_email@example.com"
```

This produces two files, `id_ed25519`, the private key which must never be shared, and `id_ed25519.pub`, the public key, which gets copied to a server's `~/.ssh/authorized_keys` to enable passwordless login. Private keys should always have restrictive file permissions:

```bash
chmod 600 ~/.ssh/id_ed25519
```

It is also good practice to protect a private key file with its own passphrase, so that even if the file is stolen it cannot be used without that passphrase too.

SSH agent forwarding lets you use your local private key to authenticate to further servers from a machine you have already connected to, without ever copying the key onto that intermediate machine:

```bash
ssh -A user@server1
ssh user@server2
```

## Common Cryptographic Attacks

It is worth grouping together the main categories of attack against cryptographic systems, since they come up throughout the rest of this note in different forms. A brute force attack tries every possible key or password until one works, and its feasibility depends entirely on key length and available computing power. A dictionary attack is a smarter version of brute force that only tries likely passwords from a wordlist rather than every possible combination. A rainbow table attack, already covered above, uses precomputed hash lookups instead of computing hashes on the fly. A birthday attack exploits the mathematics of collisions, it turns out that finding any two inputs that collide is far easier than finding a specific collision for a given input, because of how probability scales with the number of attempts, which is why hash output lengths need to be roughly double the desired security level to resist this kind of attack. A man-in-the-middle attack intercepts communication between two parties, which is exactly what certificate verification and key pinning are designed to prevent. A side-channel attack does not attack the mathematics of the algorithm at all, it attacks its physical implementation, for example measuring how long an operation takes or how much power a chip draws can leak information about a secret key. A downgrade attack tricks two parties into using a weaker, older version of a protocol or cipher that is easier to break, which is why modern systems disable old protocol versions like SSLv3 and weak ciphers entirely rather than just deprioritizing them. A replay attack captures a legitimate, validly signed or encrypted message and resends it later to trick a system into repeating an action, which is defended against using techniques like timestamps, nonces, and sequence numbers.

## JSON Web Tokens (JWT)

JWTs are a very common practical use of cryptography in modern web applications and APIs, so they are worth understanding here even though they are not a cipher themselves. A JWT is a compact, URL safe token made of three Base64-encoded parts separated by dots, a header describing the algorithm used, a payload containing claims such as a user ID and expiry time, and a signature. The signature is computed over the header and payload using either a symmetric algorithm like HMAC-SHA256, where the server that issues the token and the server that validates it share a secret, or an asymmetric algorithm like RSA or ECDSA, where the issuer signs with a private key and anyone can verify with the corresponding public key. This signature is what allows a server to trust that a token it receives was genuinely issued by it and has not been tampered with, without needing to look it up in a database. It is worth remembering that the header and payload of a JWT are only encoded, not encrypted, so anyone can read the contents of a JWT, they just cannot forge a valid signature for altered contents without the key.

## Post-Quantum Cryptography

A large enough quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would be able to break RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and ECC efficiently, since their security relies on mathematical problems that classical computers find hard but quantum computers would not. This does not affect symmetric algorithms like AES nearly as badly, since the best known quantum attack against them, Grover's algorithm, only roughly halves the effective key length, so AES-256 would still offer AES-128 level security, which remains fine. In response, NIST has been standardizing new post-quantum algorithms built on different mathematical problems believed to resist quantum attacks, such as lattice-based schemes like CRYSTALS-Kyber for key exchange and CRYSTALS-Dilithium for signatures. This is still an evolving area but organizations handling long-lived sensitive data are already starting to migrate toward these algorithms or hybrid schemes that combine classical and post-quantum methods.

## Steganography

Steganography is the practice of concealing information inside another medium, such as an image, audio file, video, or piece of text, so that the very existence of the hidden data is hidden, not just its content. This is the key difference from cryptography, encryption makes data unreadable but its presence is obvious, whereas steganography tries to make the hidden data invisible in the first place. The two are often combined, you might encrypt a secret message first and then hide the resulting ciphertext inside an image, giving you both confidentiality and concealment.

Steganography can be applied to several kinds of carrier media. Text steganography hides a message inside another text document. Image steganography hides data inside the pixel data of a digital image. Audio steganography hides data inside an audio file. Video steganography hides data inside a video file. Network steganography hides data inside the structure or timing of network packets themselves.

### Steghide Practical

To embed a secret file inside an image using a password:

```bash
steghide embed -cf image.jpg -ef secret.txt -p mypassword
```

Here `-cf` is the carrier file that will hold the hidden data, `-ef` is the file to hide, and `-p` sets the password needed to retrieve it later.

To extract the hidden file back out:

```bash
steghide extract -sf image.jpg -p mypassword
```

Here `-sf` is the stego file, meaning the carrier file that contains hidden data.

To check whether a file appears to have anything embedded in it without extracting it:

```bash
steghide info image.jpg
```

Steghide also works with audio files. Since it generally expects an uncompressed format, an MP3 typically needs converting to WAV first:

```bash
ffmpeg -i audio.mp3 -acodec pcm_s16le -ar 44100 audio.wav
steghide embed -cf audio.wav -ef secret.txt
steghide info audio.wav
steghide extract -sf audio.wav
```

If Steghide does not handle a particular audio format well, a manual alternative is to convert the secret text to speech, mix it into the cover audio at very low volume or in an unused frequency range, and extract it later using an editor like Audacity to isolate the hidden layer.

Common steganography tools beyond Steghide include OpenStego, OutGuess, Hide n Shoot, and QuickStego, each with slightly different strengths depending on the carrier format.

The main advantage of steganography is that it hides the fact that any secret communication is happening at all, which is valuable in situations where even the presence of encrypted traffic would draw unwanted attention. Its main limitation is that steganalysis techniques can statistically detect that a file has been tampered with, even without recovering the hidden content, and the amount of data that can be hidden is limited by the size and nature of the carrier medium, and simple operations like recompressing an image can destroy the hidden data entirely.

| Aspect | Steganography | Cryptography |
|--------|---------------|---------------|
| Goal | Hides the existence of data | Scrambles data to make it unreadable |
| Detection | Hard to detect if done well | Presence of encryption is obvious |
| Typical use | Covert communication, watermarking | Confidentiality, integrity, authentication |

## Data Protection Mechanisms

Beyond specific algorithms, cryptography underpins a range of broader data protection strategies used in real systems, split roughly into hardware-based and software-based approaches.

Hardware-based mechanisms tend to be more resistant to attack because they cannot easily be bypassed by malware or remote exploitation the way software can. Full disk encryption implemented at the hardware level encrypts an entire drive transparently, so data is automatically encrypted before being written and decrypted when read, with no user intervention needed once set up, and this protects a lost or stolen laptop's data since it is unreadable without the correct credentials. Hardware-encrypted USB drives work the same way, encrypting data on the fly and requiring authentication before the contents can be accessed, useful for secure portable storage and file transfer.

Software-based mechanisms are more flexible and cheaper to deploy but can be more exposed to software-level attacks like malware or ransomware. Encryption software covers full disk encryption tools like BitLocker and FileVault, file or folder level encryption tools like VeraCrypt, and end-to-end encrypted messaging apps like Signal, where only the sender and receiver, not even the service provider, can read the message content. Password management software stores credentials in an encrypted vault, generating and storing strong unique passwords so users do not have to reuse weak ones across services. Secure file transfer software encrypts data during transmission using protocols like SFTP or FTPS, ensuring both confidentiality and integrity of files moving across a network, which matters heavily in regulated industries like finance and healthcare.

## Data Masking

Data masking is the process of obfuscating sensitive data so it remains usable for testing, development, or analysis, without exposing the real underlying values. The goal is to keep data realistic and functionally useful while making sure nobody working in a non-production environment can see actual confidential information.

There are several techniques. Static data masking creates a permanently masked copy of a dataset for use outside production. Dynamic data masking masks data on the fly, based on the requesting user's role, without altering the original stored data at all. Deterministic masking always maps the same original value to the same masked value, preserving relationships in the data, while non-deterministic masking uses random replacement values with no predictable relationship to the original. Substitution replaces sensitive values with realistic but fake ones that preserve the format. Shuffling randomly rearranges values within a column so the individual values are all real but no longer attached to the correct row. Date shifting alters dates by a random offset while preserving their format. Nulling out simply replaces sensitive fields with empty values when the actual content is not needed at all.

Data masking helps with regulatory compliance under frameworks like GDPR, HIPAA, and PCI-DSS, and reduces the risk of a data breach exposing real sensitive information, since even a leak of masked data reveals nothing usable. Its downsides are that it can be complex to implement consistently across large systems, some real-time masking techniques introduce performance overhead, and masking alone is not a complete security solution, it needs to be combined with proper encryption and access controls rather than relied on by itself.

## Data Erasure

Data erasure is the process of permanently and irreversibly removing data from a storage device so that it cannot be recovered by any means, including forensic tools. This is fundamentally different from simply deleting a file or formatting a drive, both of which typically just remove the reference to the data while the underlying bits remain physically present on the disk and recoverable with the right tools.

Proper data erasure guarantees complete destruction, is irreversible even against advanced forensic recovery attempts, and is often required for legal compliance when sensitive data must be permanently destroyed at the end of its lifecycle. It can be carried out through specialized wiping software that overwrites storage multiple times, or through physical destruction of the storage medium itself, such as shredding or degaussing a hard drive.

The main advantages are strong protection of sensitive data at end of life, easier regulatory compliance, and where physical destruction is used, safer environmentally responsible disposal of old hardware. The limitations are that some older or faulty storage media may retain recoverable residual data even after software wiping, large scale software erasure can be time consuming, and physical destruction methods add extra cost, particularly at scale.

## Biometric Security

Biometric security uses unique physiological or behavioral characteristics to verify a person's identity, on the basis that these traits are difficult to replicate or steal compared to something like a password.

Fingerprint recognition uses the unique ridge and valley patterns on a fingertip and is extremely common in smartphones and access control systems. Facial recognition analyzes distinguishing facial features such as the distance between the eyes and the shape of the nose, and is used both in device authentication and in broader surveillance systems. Iris recognition scans the unique patterns in the colored part of the eye, and because the iris is highly stable over a person's lifetime, this method is very accurate. Retina scanning captures the pattern of blood vessels at the back of the eye and is extremely accurate but requires specialized equipment. Voice recognition analyzes characteristics like pitch, tone, and speech pattern, and is used in phone-based authentication and voice assistants. Hand geometry recognition measures the physical shape and size of a hand and is less accurate than other methods but still used in some access control settings. Signature recognition analyzes the dynamics of how someone writes their signature, including pressure and speed, useful for document verification. Gait recognition, an emerging technique, identifies people by the way they walk, with potential surveillance applications. Vein recognition uses infrared light to capture the pattern of veins under the skin and is highly accurate, though the equipment for it is less commonly deployed.

Biometrics are convenient, since there is nothing to remember or carry, and they offer strong authentication accuracy since the traits are inherently unique and hard to transfer between people. The major downside is that biometric data is extremely sensitive and, unlike a password, cannot be changed if it is ever compromised, so securely storing biometric templates is critical, and systems also need to account for occasional false positives and false negatives. Common use cases include unlocking mobile devices, authenticating banking and payment transactions, controlling physical access to buildings, securing patient records in healthcare settings, and identifying individuals through surveillance systems in law enforcement contexts.
